\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Significance of the Replication-Based Validation Strategy}
\label{subsec:significance}

The replication-based validation strategy we employed provides more robust evidence for SiliQun's accuracy than relying on isolated high-fidelity demonstrations ever could. By successfully reproducing two distinct published experiments across varied hardware platforms, with results deviating by less than $\Delta F < 0.04$ from reference values, we demonstrate that SiliQun's physics engine is not only internally consistent but also externally validated against independent benchmarks.

The SiMOS replication offers particularly compelling insights into the simulator's precision. With a mean fidelity of $\bar{F} = 0.9996 \pm 0.0001$, the results align within $\Delta F = 0.0004$ of the reference experiment's reported $F > 0.999$. This tight convergence confirms that SiliQun accurately implements the ZZ Hamiltonian parameterisation, charge noise amplitude, and gate time characteristics specific to the original SiMOS device.

In contrast, the donor replication validates our physics model in a different way. The DQN algorithm's discovery of the global optimum ($F_\text{best} = 1.0000$) confirms the correct implementation of the $S$-$T_0$ Hamiltonian and hyperfine coupling. The slightly lower mean cardinal-state fidelity ($\bar{F} = 0.9748$ compared to $F > 0.99$ in He~et~al.) arises from methodological differences — specifically, our general-purpose training approach versus per-state specialisation — rather than any shortcomings in the simulator.

The GAA extension highlights SiliQun's unique ability to explore uncharted experimental terrain. Since no physical GAA device has been sufficiently characterised for gate synthesis studies, our TCAD-derived profile~\cite{Tanamoto2025} enables pioneering investigations. The results ($F_\text{best} = 0.9999$, $\bar{F}_\text{cardinal} = 0.9877$) now serve as a benchmark for future physical GAA experiments.

\subsection{Positioning Within the Quantum Simulation Landscape}
\label{subsec:positioning}

SiliQun fills a previously unoccupied niche in the quantum simulation landscape, as evidenced by three key dimensions of comparison. Notably, no other open-source simulators are both silicon-spin-specific and RL-ready, which underscores the platform's unique positioning.

Compared to general-purpose quantum simulators such as QuTiP, Qiskit Aer, and PennyLane, SiliQun significantly reduces engineering overhead. While these frameworks excel at simulating quantum dynamics, they lack Gymnasium-compatible interfaces — forcing users to manually define observation spaces, action spaces, reward functions, and episode boundaries while integrating with RL libraries. Our solution addresses this gap by packaging these components into reusable open-source software, with the PGIRS methodology (\S\ref{subsec:pgirs}) offering clear implementation guidelines for future hardware extensions.

Abstract RL environments like qgym, Qiskit Gym, and QuantumCircuitEnv face a different limitation: their depolarising noise models fail to capture the amplitude damping and dephasing channels characteristic of silicon spin qubits. Our replication experiments in \S\ref{sec:experiments} demonstrate that hardware profiles significantly impact achievable fidelity. For instance, the same DQN algorithm yielded $\bar{F}_\text{cardinal} = 0.9748$ on donors versus $0.9877$ on GAA devices under their respective Lindblad models — a variation that abstract models cannot replicate. SiliQun uniquely combines Gymnasium compatibility with first-principles Lindblad noise models for three distinct silicon technologies.

Orbell's 2024 DPhil work~\cite{Orbell2024} focuses on device tuning rather than gate synthesis, creating complementary rather than overlapping research. A natural integration path involves using Orbell's Bayesian optimisation to calibrate SiliQun's hardware profiles from experimental data, replacing our conservative manual parameters with device-specific values. This approach could bridge the gap between simulation and hardware deployment.

\subsection{PGIRS Generalisability Beyond SiMOS}
\label{subsec:generalisability}

Although developed for SiMOS qubits, the PGIRS methodology's five-phase structure (\S\ref{subsec:pgirs}) is inherently hardware-agnostic. Each phase treats the device profile $\mathcal{P}$ as an abstract parameter set, enabling different simulation environments through profile substitution without requiring architectural changes.

Our validation across three silicon technologies (SiMOS, donor, GAA) demonstrates PGIRS's intra-family generalisability. Extending it to other qubit platforms follows the same disciplined approach. Superconducting transmons (IBM, Google, Rigetti) feature well-characterised noise channels~\cite{Krantz2019} — $T_1$ relaxation, $T_2$ dephasing, and crosstalk — that map directly to SiliQun's existing Lindblad operators. Adapting SiliQun would primarily involve updating $\mathcal{P}$ and the gate set, leaving the core physics engine unchanged.

Trapped-ion systems (IonQ, Honeywell) exhibit longer coherence times but slower gates and all-to-all connectivity, a natural extension of our nearest-neighbour model. For emerging neutral atom platforms (QuEra, Pasqal), adding Rydberg blockade interactions to the Lindblad solver would enable their unique multi-qubit gates and reconfigurable topologies. Crucially, Phase 5's empirical validation protocol — using hardware-independent analytical benchmarks such as Rabi oscillations, $T_1$ decay, and Bell state fidelity — provides a systematic validation path for any new Lindblad-based profile.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{subsec:limitations}

SiliQun's current implementation has several limitations that delineate promising avenues for future development. First, the Lindblad solver operates in a time-independent regime: collapse operators and Hamiltonian parameters remain fixed throughout each episode, whereas real devices exhibit drift over minutes to hours. Incorporating time-varying noise channels — such as $1/f$ charge noise with slowly drifting amplitudes — would better approximate the non-stationary conditions encountered during extended experimental runs.

Second, the hardware profiles for donor-electron and GAA qubits are derived from published TCAD simulations and literature values rather than direct device characterisation. While the replication experiments confirm these profiles' physical plausibility, device-to-device variability means they may not accurately represent any specific fabricated qubit. Integrating Bayesian parameter estimation from experimental pulse-response data, following Orbell~et~al.'s approach~\cite{Orbell2024}, would allow SiliQun profiles to be calibrated to individual devices.

Third, the current validation focuses on single- and two-qubit gates in one-dimensional chains of up to eight qubits. Two-dimensional grid topologies, which are critical for surface-code architectures~\cite{Fowler2012}, introduce long-range crosstalk terms not yet modelled in the physics engine. Extending the Lindblad solver to handle two-dimensional connectivity and ZZ crosstalk beyond nearest neighbours is a priority for the next major release.

Finally, the deep reinforcement learning (DRL) validation experiment in \S\ref{sec:drl_agent} uses a minimal NumPy-based PPO agent to confirm interface correctness rather than to demonstrate state-of-the-art control performance. Scalability studies reveal that while DRL agents reliably solve 2-qubit target states ($F > 0.99$), performance declines for 3- and 4-qubit states under standard hyperparameter settings, motivating the development of specialised adaptive control methods. Benchmarking SiliQun against modern off-policy algorithms — SAC, TD3, and model-based approaches — on a standardised suite of gate synthesis tasks would establish SiliQun as a community benchmark, a direction we are actively pursuing in follow-on work.
