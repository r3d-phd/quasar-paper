\begin{abstract}
We present \textbf{SiliQun}, an open-source, physics-grounded simulation platform
for silicon metal-oxide-semiconductor (SiMOS) spin qubits.
SiliQun provides a Lindblad density-matrix physics engine calibrated to the
SiMOS parameter regime ($T_1 = 1$\,ms, $T_2 = 0.5$\,ms, two-qubit gate error
$p_2 = 1\%$), wrapped in a native Gymnasium-compatible interface that exposes
the simulator to any standard deep reinforcement learning (DRL) library without
modification.
Within the Software-Defined Quantum Firmware (SDQF) architecture, SiliQun
occupies the \emph{data plane}: it faithfully simulates the physical device but
does not itself implement control policy or firmware logic.
Three analytical benchmarks---Rabi oscillations, $T_1$ amplitude decay, and Bell
state fidelity versus coherence time---validate the physics engine against
closed-form solutions at machine precision ($\max\,\delta \leq 2.78 \times
10^{-16}$).
To demonstrate that the platform supports non-trivial control tasks, we train
\textbf{QUASAR~v9.51}~\cite{companion:quasar}, a seven-module SAC agent with
Bayesian Reward Function Discovery (BRFD) and DEHB hyperparameter governance,
directly on SiliQun.
QUASAR achieves gate fidelity $F = 0.9930$ on four-qubit GHZ synthesis under
full SiMOS noise in 20\,000 training steps (194\,s wall-clock), crossing the
fault-tolerance threshold of $F \geq 0.99$.
A cross-algorithm benchmark of seven DRL families (T1--T7 taxonomy) yields
fidelities spanning $0.612$ (DDPG) to $0.993$ (SAC-HMRL), demonstrating that
the platform discriminates meaningfully between algorithm classes.
Generalisation experiments on the GAA device profile achieve $F = 0.994 \pm 0.012$, confirming that SiliQun's device-profile abstraction transfers across silicon qubit architectures without retraining.
Classical optimal control baselines (GRAPE, CRAB) achieve $F = 1.000$ on the training noise level but produce fixed pulses that cannot generalise; QUASAR's policy achieves comparable noise-robust fidelity ($F_\mathrm{nr} = 0.9930$) while generalising to new targets and device profiles.
Cross-backend validation via the QuantumExecutor interface confirms that QUASAR-synthesised circuits transfer to real hardware, achieving cross-entropy benchmarking (XEB) fidelity $\geq 0.954$ on IonQ Aria-1.
Furthermore, QUASAR modules trained on SiliQun serve as building blocks for
\textbf{ANDROMEDA}~\cite{companion:andromeda}, a hierarchical quantum firmware
agent that orchestrates multiple modules to construct 20-qubit entangled states
covering the $d = 3$ surface code footprint.
SiliQun is released under the MIT licence and is pip-installable as a Qiskit
\texttt{BackendV2}-compatible provider, enabling immediate integration with the
broader Qiskit ecosystem.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{The Firmware Gap in the Quantum Computing Stack}
\label{subsec:firmware_gap}

Silicon metal-oxide-semiconductor (SiMOS) spin qubits have emerged as a leading
candidate for scalable quantum computing, offering coherence times approaching
$T_1 \sim 1$\,ms and $T_2 \sim 0.5$\,ms in recent demonstrations~\cite{Xue2022,
Noiri2022,Philips2022,Steinacker2023}, compatibility with standard CMOS
fabrication, and a nearest-neighbour qubit topology that maps naturally onto
one-dimensional quantum circuit architectures.
Yet the very precision that makes SiMOS qubits attractive also makes their
control exceptionally sensitive to noise.
Charge noise, hyperfine coupling fluctuations, and residual spin-orbit
interaction degrade gate fidelity in ways that are difficult to anticipate from
first principles alone~\cite{Veldhorst2015,Fogarty2018}.

Between the physical hardware layer and high-level quantum algorithms lies a
critical but under-theorised abstraction that we term the \emph{firmware gap}:
the layer responsible for translating abstract gate operations into
device-specific, noise-aware control policies.
In classical computing, firmware (BIOS/UEFI) provides hardware-intimate
abstraction, real-time constraint enforcement, and updateable control logic
between silicon and the operating system.
Quantum computing lacks an equivalent layer.
Quantum compilers (Qiskit, Cirq, tket) operate at the gate-circuit level and
apply hardware-agnostic optimisations; quantum error correction operates at the
logical qubit level; but the layer that learns device-specific noise profiles
and synthesises physically robust gate sequences---the quantum firmware
layer---does not yet exist as a standardised, reusable component.

Addressing the firmware gap requires two prerequisites that have not previously
been satisfied together: a physics-accurate, device-specific simulation
environment that can serve as the training substrate for a DRL agent, and a
hierarchical orchestration framework that composes trained policies into
firmware-level behaviours.
This paper addresses the first prerequisite.
SiliQun is the simulation platform (data plane); QUASAR~\cite{companion:quasar}
is the DRL control agent (control plane); and ANDROMEDA~\cite{companion:andromeda}
is the hierarchical firmware orchestrator that composes QUASAR modules into
the full firmware layer.

\subsection{The SDQF Architecture}
\label{subsec:sdqf}

We propose the \textbf{Software-Defined Quantum Firmware} (SDQF) architecture
as a principled answer to the firmware gap.
Inspired by software-defined networking's separation of routing logic from
packet forwarding~\cite{McKeown2008}, SDQF separates \emph{policy synthesis}
(the control plane) from \emph{physics simulation} (the data plane):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Data plane (SiliQun):} A physics-accurate Lindblad simulation
    engine that models the quantum device. The data plane is hardware-specific
    but policy-agnostic: it faithfully simulates the noise environment without
    prescribing how to control it.
  \item \textbf{Control plane (QUASAR):} A DRL agent that learns gate synthesis
    policies by interacting with the data plane through a standard Gymnasium
    interface. The control plane is policy-specific but hardware-agnostic: it
    can be retrained on any data plane that implements the Gymnasium API.
  \item \textbf{Firmware layer (ANDROMEDA):} A hierarchical orchestration agent
    that composes control-plane policies (individual QUASAR modules) into
    higher-level firmware behaviours, constructing multi-qubit entangled states
    that span the $d = 3$ surface code footprint. ANDROMEDA is the firmware in
    the classical sense: it provides reconfigurable, updateable control logic
    that bridges device physics and logical qubit operations.
\end{itemize}

This paper focuses on the \textbf{data plane}: SiliQun as a simulation platform
and the Physics-Grounded RL Simulation (PGIRS) methodology that governs its
design and validation.
QUASAR and ANDROMEDA are described in companion papers~\cite{companion:quasar,
companion:andromeda}; they appear here as validation evidence that the data
plane supports non-trivial control tasks at scale.

\subsection{Limitations of Existing Frameworks}
\label{subsec:intro_limitations}

No existing open-source framework satisfies both conditions required for
SDQF data-plane implementation: physical accuracy for SiMOS devices and
a standard RL interface.
QuTiP~\cite{Lambert2024} provides a comprehensive Lindblad solver but offers
no Gymnasium interface and is not designed for RL training loops.
Qiskit Pulse~\cite{Alexander2020} provides pulse-level control for IBM
superconducting hardware but has no SiMOS device model.
C3~\cite{Wittler2021} supports closed-loop optimal control via GRAPE and
CMA-ES but does not implement standard RL algorithms.
TensorFlow Quantum~\cite{Broughton2020} supports hybrid quantum-classical ML
via Cirq integration but targets gate-model circuits rather than device-level
spin qubit physics.
Gymnasium-compatible RL environments for quantum circuit synthesis (qgym~\cite{
VanDerLinde2023}, Qiskit Gym~\cite{QiskitGym2024}) use abstract depolarising
noise rather than first-principles Lindblad dynamics, making them unsuitable
for SiMOS-specific policy training.
The Oxford DPhil work of Orbell~\cite{Orbell2024} addresses SiMOS device
tuning and calibration via Bayesian optimisation and pulse-level optimal
control, but operates at the device characterisation layer rather than the
gate synthesis layer; it provides no open-source Gymnasium environment.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{subsec:contributions}

This paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{SiliQun physics engine:} A Lindblad density-matrix simulator
    calibrated to the SiMOS parameter regime, validated against three
    analytical benchmarks at machine precision, with a 93$\times$ GPU
    speedup enabling practical RL training throughput.

  \item \textbf{Gymnasium-compatible interface:} A fully compliant
    \texttt{SiliQunEnv} environment with a discrete 11-token gate action
    space, a 16-element density-matrix state representation, and a
    four-component reward function whose weights are discovered by BRFD.

  \item \textbf{PGIRS methodology:} A five-phase engineering discipline
    (Noise-First Design, Gymnasium Compliance, Reward Engineering,
    Hierarchical Decomposition, Empirical Validation) that governs the
    design, implementation, and validation of physics-grounded RL
    simulation platforms.

  \item \textbf{Cross-algorithm benchmark:} A systematic evaluation of
    seven DRL algorithm families (T1--T7 taxonomy) on the 4-qubit GHZ
    synthesis task under SiMOS noise, providing the first comparative
    benchmark of DRL algorithms on a physically calibrated SiMOS simulator.

  \item \textbf{Cross-backend validation:} A QuantumExecutor interface
    that dispatches SiliQun-trained circuits to four backends (SiliQun,
    Qiskit Aer, FakeSherbrooke, IonQ Aria-1), demonstrating
    hardware-portable policy transfer with XEB fidelity $\geq 0.954$ on
    real quantum hardware.

  \item \textbf{GAA device generalisation:} Experimental validation that
    QUASAR policies trained on the GAA device profile achieve $F = 0.994
    \pm 0.012$ on 2-qubit Bell synthesis, confirming that SiliQun's
    device-profile abstraction transfers across silicon qubit architectures.

  \item \textbf{Classical optimal control comparison:} A direct comparison
    of GRAPE and CRAB against QUASAR, demonstrating that DRL policies
    achieve comparable noise-robust fidelity ($F_\mathrm{nr} = 0.9930$
    vs.~GRAPE's $0.9966$) while providing generalisation that fixed-pulse
    methods cannot offer.

  \item \textbf{Qiskit BackendV2 provider:} A pip-installable package
    (\texttt{siliqun-provider}) that registers SiliQun as a Qiskit
    \texttt{BackendV2}-compatible backend, enabling immediate integration
    with the Qiskit ecosystem and standard \texttt{transpile()} workflows.
\end{enumerate}
