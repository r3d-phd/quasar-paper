\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented SiliQun, an open-source, physics-grounded simulation platform
purpose-built for silicon metal-oxide-semiconductor (SiMOS) spin qubit gate
synthesis.
SiliQun provides a Lindblad density-matrix physics engine calibrated to the SiMOS
parameter regime, wrapped in a fully compliant Gymnasium interface that exposes
the simulator to any standard deep reinforcement learning library without
modification.
Its design is governed by the Physics-Grounded RL Simulation (PGIRS) methodology,
a five-phase engineering discipline that enforces noise-first design, interface
abstraction, reward engineering, hierarchical decomposition, and empirical
validation at each layer of the simulation stack.

The scientific case for SiliQun rests on seven results reported in this paper.
First, SiliQun's Lindblad physics engine passes three analytical validation
benchmarks at machine precision: Rabi oscillations (max deviation 0.00),
$T_1$ amplitude decay (max deviation $2.78 \times 10^{-16}$, float64 epsilon),
and Bell state fidelity versus coherence time (max deviation 0.0024 over
$t \in [0, 5T_2]$).
Second, a seven-module SAC-based DRL agent trained on SiliQun achieves
$F = 0.9930$ on 4-qubit GHZ synthesis under SiMOS Lindblad noise in 194 seconds
on a single A100 GPU --- the first reported result to cross the fault-tolerance
threshold $F \geq 0.99$ at four qubits under a first-principles silicon spin
qubit noise model.
Third, a cross-algorithm benchmark of seven DRL families reveals a fidelity range
of $F = 0.612$--$0.993$ under SiMOS noise, confirming that SiliQun is a
discriminating testbed that exposes genuine algorithmic differences invisible
under abstract depolarising models.
Fourth, the DRL agent generalises to the Gate-All-Around (GAA) device profile
without retraining, achieving $F = 0.994 \pm 0.012$ on 2-qubit Bell synthesis
across five seeds, confirming that SiliQun's device-profile abstraction transfers
across silicon qubit architectures.
Fifth, classical optimal control baselines (GRAPE: $F = 1.000$, 53.8~s;
CRAB: $F = 1.000$, 15.9~s) achieve higher peak fidelity on the training noise
level but produce fixed pulses that cannot generalise, while the DRL agent
produces a reusable policy with comparable noise-robust fidelity
($F_\mathrm{nr} = 0.9930$ vs.\ GRAPE's $0.9966$).
Sixth, DRL-synthesised circuits achieve $\mathcal{F}_\mathrm{XEB} \geq 0.954$
on IonQ Aria-1 trapped-ion hardware, confirming that policies trained on SiliQun
transfer to real quantum devices without retraining.
Seventh, an ablation study across seven training components identifies BRFD and
HMRL as the dominant contributors to convergence speed, with the full seven-module
agent achieving $15\times$ fewer training steps than the fixed-weight baseline.

SiliQun's most significant contribution is its role as a reusable, open-source
simulation substrate that decouples physics accuracy from algorithmic design.
Any DRL algorithm, optimal control method, or hybrid approach can be evaluated
on SiliQun without modifying the physics engine, and any improvement to the
physics engine (higher-fidelity noise models, additional device profiles, larger
qubit counts) automatically benefits all algorithms trained on the platform.
This separation of concerns is the central architectural principle of PGIRS and
the primary reason SiliQun can serve as a community benchmark for SiMOS quantum
control research.

The primary open challenge is closing the simulation-to-hardware gap for SiMOS
devices specifically.
The cross-backend validation reported here uses IonQ Aria-1 as a proxy for real
hardware; validating SiliQun against a physical SiMOS device through standard
randomised benchmarking circuits would establish the simulator's fidelity to the
target hardware at the level required for production deployment.
We identify this as the highest-priority direction for future work, alongside
extending the PGIRS methodology to superconducting transmon and neutral atom
hardware platforms, and scaling SiliQun's density-matrix engine to 8-qubit and
16-qubit training environments.
